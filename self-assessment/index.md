# Professional Self-Assessment
Completing the Computer Science program and developing my CS 499 ePortfolio have given me the opportunity to look back at how my technical skills, problem-solving approach, and professional goals have developed over time. Throughout the program, I worked with several areas of computer science, including software engineering, data structures and algorithms, databases, mobile development, testing, security, and technical communication. The capstone brought these areas together by requiring me to revisit earlier work, evaluate it critically, and improve it using skills I developed later in the program. This process helped me recognize that one of my strengths is not simply building a working solution, but examining an existing system, identifying weaknesses, and making practical improvements that increase its reliability, maintainability, security, or usefulness.
The program has also helped shape my professional interests. I am especially interested in artificial intelligence, automation, workflow development, and practical technology solutions that make everyday technical and business processes easier to manage. My coursework has helped me connect those interests to real development work, such as building data-driven applications, improving validation, and strengthening application and data security. These areas require more than knowing a specific language or tool. They require understanding the problem being solved, choosing appropriate technologies, considering design trade-offs, protecting data, testing the result, and communicating technical decisions clearly. These are skills I expect to keep building as I continue working with AI-assisted tools, automated workflows, cloud-based systems, and full stack applications.
An important part of my development has been learning how computer science work fits into collaborative environments. Much of my capstone implementation work was completed individually, but the work still had to be understandable to someone reviewing it later. That matters to me because I am interested in technology work where software, IT support, automation, and documentation all connect. Code must be organized, technical decisions must be explained, and solutions must address real user needs rather than only meeting a checklist of requirements. While I did not have extensive team-based implementation work in the capstone itself, the program still required me to practice communication through discussions, peer-facing explanations, documentation, and project presentations. These experiences helped me think beyond my own code and consider how another developer, instructor, employer, or stakeholder would understand the purpose and value of a technical solution.
Communicating with stakeholders is closely connected to this collaborative mindset. Throughout the program, I learned that technical accuracy is only one part of professional communication. The level of detail and terminology must also fit the audience and purpose. In CS 499, the code review required oral and visual communication, while the enhancement narratives required written explanations of technical decisions and learning. Developing the GitHub Pages ePortfolio added another practical communication challenge because I had to present projects such as Weigh to Go, the ABCU Advising Assistance Program, and the Grazioso Salvare dashboard in a way that an instructor, employer, or technical reviewer could understand without digging through every source file first. These experiences improved my ability to explain not only what a system does, but also why a design decision matters.
My work with data structures and algorithms strengthened my ability to think about how software processes and validates information. The Computer Science program taught me that choosing an algorithm or data structure involves trade-offs involving complexity, efficiency, readability, and the requirements of the problem. During the capstone, I applied this thinking while reviewing existing program behavior and determining where additional validation was needed without unnecessarily replacing structures that were already appropriate. More broadly, this reflects a skill I developed throughout the program: evaluating an existing solution before deciding whether greater complexity would actually improve it. I have become more comfortable separating improvements that provide measurable value from changes that simply make a solution more complicated.
Software engineering coursework also changed how I approach application design. Earlier in the program, I tended to focus primarily on whether an application produced the expected result. As I progressed, I became more aware of organization, separation of responsibilities, maintainability, testing, and the ability to extend software later. Mobile development, object-oriented programming, and the work completed during the capstone reinforced the importance of creating code that can be understood and maintained rather than treating functionality as the only measure of success. The enhancement process required me to look at older projects from the perspective of a more experienced developer, which made the growth in my design decisions easier to recognize.
Database work provided another important part of my development. Working with MongoDB and data-driven applications required me to consider how applications connect to persistent data, how queries are structured, how data is validated, and how database design affects application behavior and performance. During CS 499, I expanded this understanding by working with configurable database connections, query validation, indexing, testing, and protections against dangerous update and delete operations. The experience reinforced that database development involves much more than storing and retrieving records. Reliability, performance, portability, and security all need to be considered together when designing a useful data layer.
Security has become an increasingly important part of how I evaluate software. The program helped me understand that security should not be treated as a feature added after development is complete. It should influence design decisions from the beginning. In the capstone, this mindset appeared in decisions such as removing hard-coded database credentials, validating fields, operators, and value types, and preventing update or delete operations from running with empty filters. I also identified password hashing as a future improvement for my mobile application rather than claiming it was implemented when it was not. These experiences strengthened my understanding that professional development requires identifying security limitations honestly, reducing unnecessary risk, validating external input, and protecting data and resources.
Work outside the three enhanced artifacts also contributed to my professional growth. In CS 465 Full Stack Development, I built the Travlr Getaways application using the MEAN stack, including MongoDB, Express, Angular, and Node.js. That project gave me experience connecting a customer-facing website, an authenticated administrative single-page application, REST API endpoints, JSON data, and database operations into one complete system. It also strengthened my understanding of testing across layers because I used tools such as Postman, MongoDB Compass, Chrome DevTools, and the browser to verify that data moved correctly between the frontend, backend, and database.
The CS 499 code review, GitHub Pages ePortfolio, and course journals also contributed to my development. The code review required me to evaluate existing systems before changing them and communicate an improvement strategy. Building and maintaining the ePortfolio required attention to public accessibility, navigation, file organization, and presentation. The course journals encouraged me to connect my degree work to technology areas I follow closely, including agentic artificial intelligence, digital twins, cloud computing, and generative AI. These topics relate directly to my interest in automation and workflow improvement because they show how computer science can be used to reduce repetitive work, support better decision-making, and connect systems in more useful ways.
The three technical artifacts that follow this self-assessment demonstrate different parts of this development while working together as a broader representation of my computer science abilities. The first artifact is the Weigh to Go Android application originally developed in CS 360. Its enhancement focuses on software design and engineering by improving the organization of the application using a simplified MVVM-style structure involving activities, ViewModels, a repository, and the existing database helper. This artifact demonstrates my growth in application architecture, separation of responsibilities, and maintainability.
The second artifact is the ABCU Advising Assistance Program from CS 300. This enhancement represents algorithms and data structures and focuses on strengthening how course information is validated. Required-field validation, duplicate-course detection, and prerequisite-integrity validation were added while preserving valid catalog loading, sorting, course lookup, prerequisite display, and menu behavior. This artifact demonstrates my ability to analyze program logic, add validation without disrupting correct behavior, and consider algorithmic design choices and trade-offs.
The third artifact is the Grazioso Salvare animal-shelter dashboard from CS 340. This enhancement represents database development and demonstrates some of the strongest security and testing improvements in the portfolio. The enhanced version includes configurable MongoDB connection handling, removal of hard-coded credentials, validation of query fields and operators, protection against unsafe update and delete operations, a compound MongoDB index, compatibility updates, and automated connection and query-validation testing. Because the original course database was no longer available, I also used a representative local test database containing the fields necessary to verify the enhanced dashboard behavior. This allowed the enhancement to be tested without misrepresenting the test data as the original course database.
Together, these artifacts demonstrate a progression from application structure, to algorithmic reasoning and validation, to database reliability, performance, testing, and security. They also show that my growth in computer science has involved more than learning additional languages or technologies. I have become more deliberate about how I analyze problems, evaluate existing solutions, make design trade-offs, test changes, protect data, and explain my decisions. The ePortfolio provides evidence of that development through both the technical work and the reflections that accompany it.
As I complete the Computer Science program, I feel better prepared to continue developing as a technology professional. I understand that completing the degree is not the end of learning in this field. Technologies and development practices will continue to change, particularly in areas that already interest me, such as artificial intelligence, automation, cloud systems, secure application development, and data-driven tools. My goal is to continue building on the foundation developed throughout the program while improving my ability to create technology solutions that are secure, reliable, maintainable, and genuinely useful. The capstone has given me a clearer understanding of the skills I can bring to future opportunities and the areas in which I want to continue growing.
